American burying beetle

Nicrophorus americanus

A large black-and-orange carrion beetle that buries small carcasses to feed its young. It is thought to be gone from Alabama.

Thought to be gone from Alabama. This insect is on the federal list and once lived in the state, but it is not known here today.

What it looks like

A shiny black beetle 1 to 1.8 inches long, marked in orange-red: two scalloped spots on each wing cover, a patch on the head, orange clubs on the antennae, and a large mark on the shield behind the head. That last mark is the sure sign. No other burying beetle in North America has it.

Where it lives

It is not particular. It has been found in wet meadows, grasslands, pastures, shrublands, and open woods. What it needs is a supply of small dead birds and mammals.

How it lives

Adults are active at night from late spring to early fall, and can fly as far as 18 miles in a night in search of carrion. A male and a female bury a small carcass together, shape it into a ball, and raise 12 to 18 young on it, feeding the larvae themselves. Adults live about a year.

In Alabama

The beetle once ranged over at least 35 states. The University of Alabama's museums hold American burying beetles in the Löding Beetle Collection, gathered across Alabama, and chiefly around Mobile, between 1900 and 1930. The Encyclopedia of Alabama calls the species possibly extirpated in the state, and says surveys of suitable habitat are needed to be sure.

Why it is at risk

The Service now names a warming climate as the greatest threat to most surviving populations, along with the loss of habitat to development and cropland. Why the beetle vanished from most of the East is less certain. One leading idea is that changes to the land left too few carcasses of the right size.

Notes

Listed as endangered in 1989, and reclassified as threatened on October 15, 2020 (85 FR 65241). A reintroduced population in southwestern Missouri is listed separately, as an experimental population.
