Mitchell's satyr

Neonympha mitchellii mitchellii

A small brown butterfly of shaded sedge wetlands, unknown in Alabama until a chance sighting in 2000.

What it looks like

A small brown butterfly with a wingspan of about 1.75 inches. The upper sides of the wings are plain. The undersides carry a row of round black eyespots ringed in yellow, set between two reddish-orange lines, and the spots on the hindwing are the largest. Its flight is low and bobbing. Several other satyrs look much like it, so the Alabama Butterfly Atlas advises weighing the place and the season along with the markings.

Where it lives

Wetlands where sunny sedge meadow meets shaded swamp forest. In Alabama this ground is usually made, and kept open, by beavers over many years. In Michigan and Indiana the butterfly lives in fens fed by mineral-rich springs.

How it lives

The caterpillars feed on sedges, and in Alabama also on several grasses, including the invasive Japanese stilt grass. Half-grown caterpillars pass the winter and finish growing in spring. Adults live two to three weeks. They seldom visit flowers, avoid direct sun, and are most active late in the afternoon and on warm, overcast days.

In Alabama

First seen in the state on June 3, 2000, along a Bibb County roadside, more than 500 miles from the nearest population then known. Searches since have found small, scattered colonies in Bibb, Fayette, Hale, and Tuscaloosa Counties, most of them in the Oakmulgee Ranger District of Talladega National Forest.

Why it is at risk

Loss and draining of wetlands, changes to the flow of water, and plant succession: as shrubs and trees close in, a site stops supporting the butterfly. Its habitat in Alabama is temporary by nature, and some of the first sites found no longer hold it. Pesticides, and the small size and isolation of the colonies, add to the risk.

Notes

The butterfly was listed by emergency rule on June 25, 1991 (56 FR 28825), and by final rule the next year. The southern colonies, found in Alabama, Mississippi, and Virginia since the late 1990s, have been studied to learn whether they belong with the Michigan and Indiana butterflies or are a subspecies of their own. The Fish & Wildlife Service includes them in the range of Mitchell's satyr.
