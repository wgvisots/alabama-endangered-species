Report a sighting

If you come across one of these species, a careful report can help the people who are working to keep it. This page says how to look without doing harm, what to write down, and who would like to hear of it.

First, do no harm

  • Watch and photograph from where you stand. Do not touch, catch, move, or feed an animal, and do not pick, dig, or press a plant. Federal law forbids harassing a listed animal, as well as killing or collecting one.
  • Leave the place as you found it. Many of the animals on this site shelter or nest under stones and logs, so do not turn them over. Keep out of caves.
  • Around manatees, the Dauphin Island Sea Lab asks boaters to cut the motor and stay at least 100 feet away. Anything that changes how a manatee behaves counts as harassment.
  • On a beach at night, the Fish & Wildlife Service asks you to put out white lights, to stay low, quiet, and at least 30 feet behind a nesting sea turtle, and to keep out of marked nests.
  • Keep the exact place to yourself. Give it to the scientists named below, and leave it out of anything you post in public, because rare plants and animals are taken by collectors. A phone or camera may write the location into the photo file, so check before you share one.

What to write down

The Alabama Natural Heritage Program's report form is a fair guide to what is useful.

  • The date, and who saw it.
  • The place: the county, the coordinates and how you got them, and directions that would bring someone else to the spot.
  • What you saw, how many, and whether they were young or grown.
  • The habitat, and anything that seemed to threaten it.
  • Whether you have a photograph. A clear photo is the best evidence there is. Many of these species have common look-alikes, and telling them apart can take a specialist.

Who would like to hear of it

For any rare plant or animal

  • Alabama Natural Heritage Program, Auburn University auburn.edu
    It asks for sighting reports of Alabama's imperiled species, checks them, and adds them to its records. Fill in its rare species report form and send it to the address on the form.
  • iNaturalist inaturalist.org
    A free site and phone app. You post a photo, and other naturalists help to name it. The Alabama Cooperative Extension System recommends it for sightings of native species, rare ones included. For a species that could be harmed if its whereabouts were known, iNaturalist hides the true place from the public and shows a random point in a box about a dozen miles on a side. You can hide the place of any sighting of your own in the same way.
  • U.S. Fish & Wildlife Service: Species Point of Contact in Alabama fws.gov
    The Service names the biologist who answers for each listed species in the state. That is the person to tell if you find something surprising, such as a species thought to be gone from Alabama.

On the coast

  • Manatee Sighting Network, Dauphin Island Sea Lab disl.edu
    Report any manatee, at any hour, on the network's form or at 1-866-493-5803. The same number takes reports of a manatee that is hurt, stranded, or dead.
  • Sea turtles outdooralabama.com
    For a nesting turtle, or one that is stranded or dead, call 1-866-732-8878, which spells 1-866-SEA-TURTLE.
  • Dolphins and whales outdooralabama.com
    These are outside the scope of this site, but a stranded one should be reported to the Alabama Marine Mammal Stranding Network at 1-877-942-5343.

If you see harm being done

Alabama's conservation officers take reports of fish and wildlife violations at any hour on the Operation GameWatch line, 1-800-272-4263. Calls are confidential. Note what you can from a distance, such as the time, the place, and a description of the people and their vehicle or boat, and do not confront anyone.

What this site does with reports

Nothing. This site collects no sightings, and has no way to pass them on. If something you have seen shows that an entry here is wrong or out of date, you are welcome to open an issue on GitHub. Please do not include locations.
